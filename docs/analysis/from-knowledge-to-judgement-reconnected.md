# From Knowledge to Judgement

**A field-tested system for turning lived experience into learnable, executable judgement — built and tested over nine years, in Lagos and beyond, before it had a name this clean.**

---

## A note on what this paper is

This is the third name this body of work has had. It began in 2016 as the **Growth and Innovation System (GIS)**, built and tested through a venture called Frintern. It continued in 2019 under the name **Growth Clinic**. What follows is its current form, the **Human Operating System (HOS)**. The architecture has matured across those three names. The underlying insight has not changed: that expert judgement is not the same thing as expert knowledge, that people are far more consistent in what they actually do than in how they'd explain what they do if you asked them, and that the gap between knowing and judging can be deliberately closed rather than left to accident and tenure.

Every claim in this paper is grounded in something that actually happened — a dated pilot, a named client, a real interview, a documented failure — not in borrowed academic authority or unverified claims about tools that don't yet exist. Where something is still exploratory or unresolved, this paper says so directly, because a paper that only shows its wins isn't trustworthy enough to be worth reading.

---

## Executive Summary

In 2016, while managing a community of developers and designers in Lagos, a specific pattern kept appearing: people who joined asking beginner questions were, within weeks, helping others solve genuinely hard ones. That observation, and a separate one made around the same time — that three different Nigerians, asked about how they used star-rating apps, gave three completely different, internally consistent personal philosophies, none of which they'd have volunteered if simply asked to describe their approach — became the seed of everything that follows.

The core insight: **knowledge is what you understand; judgement is what you do with it under pressure, and people are far more reliable in the second than they are in explaining the first.** Most professional development — courses, degrees, onboarding programmes — transfers knowledge. It does not build judgement, because building judgement requires something different: high-volume exposure to varied real scenarios, fast feedback on each one, and a framework for making sense of the pattern as it emerges.

This paper describes the system built, over nine years, to close that gap — tested through paid training engagements with named clients, a measured and dated pilot result, at least one venture that didn't survive commercially despite the underlying mechanism working, and a live product currently being tested right now, with its outcome still unresolved. The system has three parts: **Instructional Deconstruction** (breaking a professional competency into a Career Recipe a newcomer can apply in thirty seconds), **Thinking Frameworks** (five domains — Design, Business, Market, User, Project — first delivered live to a real accelerator cohort in 2018), and **the Execution Loop** (tracking real execution and using deliberate scenario practice to build pattern recognition faster than unstructured experience would).

What follows is not a pitch. It is a record of what was built, what was tested, what worked, what didn't, and what's still an open question right now.

## Section 1 — The Problem: Why Knowledge Alone Wasn't Enough

### 1.1 The observation that started this

In 2016, working as Community Manager for Devcenter.co — a Nigerian community of developers, designers, and tech enthusiasts originally formed around building open-source projects together — a pattern surfaced that didn't match how professional skill development is normally described. People arriving with beginner-level questions were, within weeks, capable of helping others through complex problems. Not one standout person. A repeated pattern, across many members.

This raised a real, testable question: was this acceleration something specific to the people involved, or was it a mechanism that could be understood and deliberately reproduced for people who hadn't happened into the right community by chance?

### 1.2 The distinction that made everything downstream possible

A separate observation from the same period turned out to matter more than any single case of accelerated learning: three people, asked how they used star-rating systems, gave three genuinely different, coherent philosophies that none of them would have stated if simply asked to describe their "approach" in the abstract.

One person refused to rate a reckless, rude driver poorly, explaining: *"I can't give him a low rating na; that's where he eats. I'm not a bad person!"* — prioritising someone's livelihood over accuracy. Another, after a genuinely good meal, gave three stars rather than five: *"I give three stars to a good place where they did what they said they'd do and the experience was satisfactory... Then five stars is for God; only God is perfect."* A third simply ignored the system unless something was exceptional or notably bad.

Three real decision policies. Discoverable only by watching what people actually did, case by case — not by asking them to explain themselves. This is the load-bearing insight behind everything in this paper: **behaviour is more honest than self-report, and judgement lives in the pattern of behaviour, not in the explanation a person gives for it after the fact.**

### 1.3 Why this matters more now than it did in 2016

The pace at which a single skill set stays relevant has kept shrinking. Product cycles that once spanned years compress into quarters; roles that required deep single-discipline expertise increasingly require that expertise plus the ability to pick up adjacent domains fast. This isn't a new observation on its own — but it does mean that a mechanism for closing the knowledge-to-judgement gap faster matters more today than it did nine years ago, when the first version of this system was built to solve a smaller, more local version of the same problem: a skill gap inside one Lagos tech community, with no clear bridge between its beginners and its experts.

## Section 2 — Instructional Deconstruction: Breaking Skill Into Its Executable Core

### 2.1 The first attempt, and its honest failure

The first system built to test whether learning acceleration could be engineered deliberately was called **Learn by Doing**. It combined project-based learning (real open-source contributions, not exercises), task-based learning (large projects broken into manageable pieces), and one-on-one apprenticeship-style mentorship.

It failed in a specific, undramatised way. Learners started with real enthusiasm. After roughly two weeks, they consistently dropped off. Trying to fix this by incentivising continued participation didn't work. This is stated plainly because it's the honest starting point: the first version of this idea, tested for real, did not succeed as built.

### 2.2 What that failure led to

Rather than iterating blindly on the same format, the failure prompted a direct question: why don't successful learning communities have this drop-off problem? Two reference points anchored the answer.

**Seth Godin's altMBA**, launched in 2015, reported a **96% completion rate** against a documented online-course industry average of **4%** — a roughly 24-times difference in a metric most online education treats as an unavoidable cost of the format. Marie Schacht, CEO of Akimbo (altMBA's parent company), described the actual mechanism: *"People add the learning time to their calendars and for them, it is time with other people, not just time to learn."* Members who'd completed the course years earlier were still in touch with people from their original cohort group.

**Josh Kaufman's** 20-hours framework, from a 2013 TEDx talk, broke skill acquisition into four steps: deconstruct the skill; learn enough to self-correct; remove barriers to practice; practise for at least 20 hours.

### 2.3 Frintern, and a measured result

These two reference points were combined into a new venture, **Frintern**, later continued as **techstarta.com**. Kaufman's four steps were mapped directly to four mechanisms: skills were deconstructed using first-principles reasoning combined with real interviews with skilled practitioners, producing compressed **mental models** for each skill; free resources were curated into microlearning-sized pieces; a personalisation system matched people to skill paths suited to their natural affinities; and structured "Learning Labs" put people onto real-world projects immediately rather than isolated exercises.

**In pilot workshops run from March to May 2018**, participants using this combined system learned a new skill and used it to create something functional in **2 to 3 hours** — down from Kaufman's 20-hour baseline. This is the only hard, dated figure for Time-to-Learn-Skills (TTLS) reduction anywhere in this body of work. It has not been reproduced at larger scale since, and this paper does not claim it generalises beyond that pilot without further testing.

### 2.4 The Career Recipe format

What emerged from this process — carried forward, refined, and still central today — is the **Career Recipe**: a structured, five-part description of a single executable skill, deliberately constrained to be readable and applicable within about thirty seconds.

| Component | Purpose | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| **Domain** | Which framework it belongs to (see Section 3) | Business |
| **Skill Name** | The specific, nameable thing being taught | Identifying a leaky value proposition |
| **Trigger** | The condition that tells you to use it | A product is being used but not renewed |
| **Action Sequence** | The minimum correct steps | List what users pay for → list what they actually use → find the gap → ask whether the gap is a usage problem or a promise problem |
| **Expected Outcome** | What correct execution produces | A clear hypothesis about whether the retention problem is in the product or in the marketing |

A Recipe is not a summary of a course. It's a single move that an experienced practitioner performs automatically, rendered so a newer practitioner can start applying it before they've earned it through years of their own experience. The constraint is deliberate: if a piece of expertise can't be compressed into this format, that's usually a sign it hasn't been deconstructed far enough yet, not that the format has failed.

## Section 3 — The Five Thinking Frameworks: Tested Live, Not Designed in the Abstract

### 3.1 Where the frameworks actually came from

The five domains — **Design, Business, Market, User, Project** — were not derived from theory first and tested later. They were first delivered, as a named set, to a real, paying client: the **African Fintech Foundry (AFF)**, training the first cohort of their Fintech Accelerator Programme after organising the foundry's first demo day. One concrete outcome from that cohort: participants realised mid-programme that they'd been building products first and searching for a market to fit afterward, and needed to reverse that — understand the market, then let it shape the product. The frameworks weren't abstract theory to that cohort; they visibly changed how real founders approached a real, live decision.

This was followed by a second, independent paid engagement — brought in directly by **Hugo Obi**, founder of Maliyo Games, ahead of his team's fintech product launch, despite real reluctance and pricing friction on both sides at the outset. Maliyo's team provided direct feedback that refined the material further, and their engagement served as a genuine second test that the frameworks generalised beyond the specific context they'd first been built for.

### 3.2 Where the content of the frameworks came from

The substance behind each framework — what it's actually built to notice — comes from years of direct, situated observation of Lagos communities and markets, not from imported theory:

- **The User Framework** draws directly on the Five-Star Dilemma: the discovery that three real people held three different, coherent rating philosophies that none of them would state if asked directly — the concrete evidence behind the framework's core question, *what is the user actually trying to accomplish, not the stated goal but the underlying one.*
- **The Business and Market Frameworks** draw on TKD Farms Farmers' Market in Ikoyi, Lagos, where a vendor named S (founder of Infused Organics) described how a single customer suggestion, acted on directly, led to a collaboration with a neighbouring vendor and a new best-selling product — a live example of value being created through direct signal rather than market research reports.
- **The User and Business Frameworks** together draw on El Padrino, a Lagos taco restaurant that built a following not primarily through food but through treating returning customers like friends and staying anchored to one community — to the point that its customer base followed it through multiple relocations, and it became known locally as "the Maggi of Tacos."
- **The Market Framework's** question about unserved segments draws on Jay's Diner, which built a loyal base exclusively during 6pm–5am hours by treating the night-shift crowd as its primary focus rather than an afterthought.
- **The Design and User Frameworks** together draw on The BodyLanguage Company, a Lagos dance studio that ran near-daily free classes at bars and lounges — turning what could have been a paid service into a community experience that naturally converted casual attendees into paying students.

### 3.3 The frameworks themselves

Each framework is a set of triggering questions — what an experienced practitioner in that domain automatically asks when encountering a new situation, made explicit enough that a newer practitioner can borrow the pattern recognition before they've earned it themselves.

**Design** — What job is this designed to do, from the user's perspective rather than the designer's? Where does it create friction it didn't intend to? What's been optimised for, and what's been sacrificed in that optimisation?

**Business** — Where is value actually being created in this system? What is being exchanged for what, and is that sustainable? Who captures the value, and what gives them that position?

**Market** — Who else is solving this problem, and what does their approach reveal about what buyers actually value? Is the urgency of this problem increasing or decreasing? Where are the unserved segments, and why are they unserved?

**User** — What is the user actually trying to accomplish, not the stated goal but the underlying one? What would we need to believe about user behaviour for this decision to be correct?

**Project** — What is the critical path, and where is it most fragile? What's the minimum viable version that still tests the core assumption?

These frameworks were released as part of an openly licensed book, explicitly built to be forked, remixed, and extended by anyone applying them in a context this work hasn't reached. They are not presented as finished or complete — only as tested, live, against real decisions made by real people.

## Section 4 — The Execution Loop: What's Proven, What's Exploratory, and What's Live Right Now

This is the section where honesty matters most, because it's the section where the original version of this paper overstated what existed. Here is where things actually stand.

### 4.1 The core mechanism

The Execution Loop rests on the same insight established in Section 1.2: people are more reliable in what they do than in how they explain what they do. The loop has two intended mechanisms — tracking real-world execution as it happens, and using deliberately varied scenario practice to build pattern recognition in domains a practitioner hasn't yet earned direct experience in. Both feed a shared library of Career Recipes that grows as real execution and real scenario practice accumulate.

### 4.2 What was actually built, and its actual status

Two tools were built to test this mechanism directly: **SYD Protocol**, intended to track and reward real-world execution, and **Slop Runner**, intended as a scenario-sparring engine. **Neither has been used by a real user outside its own construction.** Both were built to help externalise and think through a concept that existed clearly enough conceptually but hadn't yet found a working shape — not to solve a demonstrated problem for a real person. This is stated plainly here because an earlier version of this paper described both as live, working tools, which was not accurate.

### 4.3 The real, current test of this mechanism: LORE

The mechanism is currently being tested for real, in a different but directly related form: **LORE**, a live product aimed at organisational knowledge transfer — specifically, capturing the judgement of an experienced employee before they leave, without requiring them to sit down and document it themselves, on the same principle established in Section 1.2 that people are more reliably observed through their decisions than through their self-description.

**LORE is not a success case. It is currently being tested, and its outcome is unresolved.** The first version received a specific, unflattering, real piece of feedback: an HR manager reviewing it asked, in effect, why they would use it at all when they could just do the same knowledge-transfer work manually. That objection wasn't deflected — it directly triggered a full revamp of the product, currently in progress, built specifically to answer that objection rather than defend the original design.

A separate internal test of the underlying extraction method — one practitioner working through the full process — produced a result that was honestly diagnosed rather than presented as a win: the output was judged "correct but generic," and that judgement was traced to two specific, fixable implementation gaps, not left as a vague sense that something was wrong. This is the standard this whole system is trying to hold itself to: when something doesn't work, find the specific reason, not a story that explains it away.

### 4.4 What this section is and isn't claiming

This paper does not claim the Execution Loop, as a mechanism, has been proven at scale. It claims: the underlying principle it rests on has held up consistently since 2016, across four different attempts to build something on top of it; one direct implementation of the principle is live and being tested right now, with a real objection already surfaced and a real response to it in progress; and two earlier attempts to build the mechanism never reached a real user, which is recorded here rather than hidden.

## Section 5 — Execution Intuition: The Target State

The goal of this system, across all three of its names, has never been to make someone a faster learner in the abstract. It's to make someone a better judge — someone who can walk into an unfamiliar situation and rapidly form a reliable hypothesis about what's actually happening and what the first right move is.

This isn't mysticism, and it isn't innate talent. Every piece of evidence in this paper — the Five-Star Dilemma, the AFF cohort's market-first realisation, the Frintern TTLS pilot, LORE's current extraction work — points to the same mechanism: judgement is built through high-volume, high-variety, high-feedback exposure to real cases, structured by a framework that helps a person make sense of the pattern as it forms, not through the accumulation of more explained knowledge. The person who has spent years watching Lagos markets, diners, and dance studios evolve into economic engines didn't develop that eye by reading about community economics — they developed it by watching hundreds of individual cases and building a working framework for what they were seeing, the exact process this paper is trying to make available to someone who hasn't had nine years to do the same.

## Section 7 — Applying This Directly

**If you're an individual practitioner:** pick one framework — ideally the one you're weakest in, not the one you already feel confident in. Spend a week noticing which of its triggering questions you wouldn't have thought to ask on your own. At the end of a real decision that went well, write it up as a Career Recipe: what triggered it, what the actual steps were, what the outcome was. This is the same process that produced the very first Recipes out of the original Frintern interviews with skilled practitioners.

**If you're building something on top of this system:** be honest, in your own materials, about what's proven versus what's proposed — the mistake this paper is correcting. A system built on nine years of real, dated, occasionally failed attempts is more credible than one dressed up with borrowed academic citations, and it doesn't need the borrowed citations to be believed.

## Appendix A — Glossary

**Career Recipe** — A five-part, thirty-second description of a single executable professional skill: Domain, Skill Name, Trigger, Action Sequence, Expected Outcome.

**Time-to-Learn-Skills (TTLS)** — The measured time from first encountering a skill to reliable, autonomous application of it. Reduced to 2–3 hours in a dated pilot (March–May 2018), from a 20-hour baseline.

**Execution Intuition** — The capacity to enter an unfamiliar situation and rapidly form a reliable working hypothesis about what matters and what to do first. The target state of this whole system.

**Thinking Framework** — A structured set of triggering questions for a professional domain. Five currently exist: Design, Business, Market, User, Project. First delivered live in 2018 at the African Fintech Foundry.

**Execution Loop** — The mechanism intended to combine real-world execution tracking with deliberate scenario practice. Currently tested through LORE, live and unresolved; two earlier attempts (SYD Protocol, Slop Runner) never reached a real user.
