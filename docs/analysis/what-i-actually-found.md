## What I actually found, and what happened to it after

At Devcenter, part of what we did was connect developers in the community to clients who needed work done. I was the middleman — the project manager sitting between the client and whoever in the community had the skill to deliver. It kept breaking in the same place: we didn't have enough skilled people. Not enough to say yes to the clients we could have had, not enough to deliver well on the ones we did take. We were losing money and losing clients for the same reason — a talent gap, not a demand gap.

And then I noticed something. People who'd been asking beginner questions in the community a few months earlier were now genuinely skilled. Not one or two of them. A pattern of them. Nobody had done anything special to make it happen. No one had pulled them aside, no extra training, no deliberate hand guiding it. It was just happening, on its own, inside the community.

That's where the real idea came from, before it had a name or a deck attached to it. If something was already teaching people that fast without anyone designing it to, what if I could find the actual mechanism — not admire it, take it apart, find the gears — and then build the same thing on purpose? If that worked, it wasn't just a fix for our talent problem. It meant you could take an ordinary community, any size, and turn it into a pipeline of skilled talent, and build a business standing exactly where I already was: in the middle, connecting that talent to the people who needed it. I remember thinking about it like how China built its wealth — not by having wealth first, but by building the industrial talent that let it manufacture everything the world needed. I wanted to do that for tech, for this new digital-infrastructure era. Except the raw material wasn't factories. It was people getting good, fast, inside a community that was already doing it without knowing it.

So I went looking for the mechanism, and what I found was learning by doing — but specific, not the vague version people usually mean by that phrase. People were contributing to real open source projects, with real stakes even if the stakes were just reputation. They'd take a task, get stuck, ask a question, someone further along would answer, they'd apply it, get feedback on what they'd built, fix it, and move to the next task already a bit better than an hour before. Do that enough times inside the same tight loop and the project starts acting like an oven — you put in something half-formed, the heat of real feedback works on it, and what comes out is more baked than what went in. What made it fast wasn't any single piece of the loop. It was how quickly the loop closed. The speed of the feedback *was* the learning.

---

Once I could see the mechanism this clearly, I started seeing it everywhere — other training programmes, other communities, quietly running some version of the same loop without naming it. The first real attempt to build it on purpose was Learn by Doing: project-based work, task-based breakdowns, one-on-one mentorship. It got real enthusiasm out of people for about two weeks, and then they dropped off, and no amount of incentivising fixed it. I didn't take that as proof the idea was wrong. I took it as proof the format was missing something the organic version had by accident.

Two things pointed at what that something was. Seth Godin's altMBA was hitting a 96% completion rate against an industry average of 4% for online courses — and Marie Schacht, who ran it, put her finger on why: "People add the learning time to their calendars and for them, it is time with other people, not just time to learn." It wasn't better content closing that gap. It was the loop staying social. And Josh Kaufman's twenty-hours talk gave a shape to the deconstruction side of it — break the skill down, learn enough to self-correct, remove the barriers to practice, then actually practise.

I built those into Frintern. Skills got broken down using real interviews with people who were good at them, turned into compressed mental models; free resources got cut into microlearning pieces; a personalisation system matched people to paths that suited what they were naturally good at; and Learning Labs put people onto real projects immediately instead of drills. In pilot workshops between March and May 2018, people were learning a skill and using it to make something functional in two to three hours, down from Kaufman's twenty. That's a real, dated number. It hasn't been tested again at any larger scale since, and I'm not going to pretend it has.

After I explained all this to Sean Burrowes, he called it "The Innovation Engine." I ended up calling it the Growth and Innovation System.

---

The frameworks came the same way — tested live, not designed in a room. The first time the five of them (Design, Business, Market, User, Project) got used as a named set was training the first cohort of the African Fintech Foundry's accelerator. One thing that came out of that cohort directly: they realised mid-programme they'd been building a product first and looking for a market afterward, and needed to flip it. That wasn't theory landing on them. That was a live decision changing because of a framework. Hugo Obi brought the same training into Maliyo Games not long after, and his team's feedback sharpened it further — a second, independent test that it wasn't a one-off fit for one room of people.

What the frameworks were actually built to notice came from years of just watching. The Five-Star Dilemma is the clearest example — three different Nigerians, asked how they used star ratings, gave three completely different, coherent philosophies, none of which they'd have volunteered if you'd simply asked them to describe their "approach." One wouldn't rate a reckless driver poorly because "that's where he eats." Another gave three stars on principle because "five stars is for God; only God is perfect." A third only rated anything if it was exceptional or terrible. That's the User Framework's actual foundation — people are far more honest in what they do than in how they'd explain what they do. TKD Farms market gave the Business and Market side of it — a vendor collaborating on a new product the moment a customer suggested it, no market research required. El Padrino gave the same thing from the loyalty side — a taco restaurant that survived multiple relocations because people followed the community, not the address. The BodyLanguage Company gave the Design side — free dance classes that turned casual attendees into paying students without anyone ever pitching them anything.

---

But here's where I think the shape of it started to drift, and I want to be honest about exactly where.

The whole point, from the Devcenter moment onward, was never "help individuals get better judgement." That's a real thing that happens inside the loop — but it was always a side effect, not the target. The target was: take a group of people with a shared problem, run them through the deconstruction and the frameworks, and have them build something *real* out of it — a product, a business, a project — that then works as a risk-free ground to keep practising on. And if that real thing actually generates value, it sustains the people who built it, and ideally the community around them too. That's the "shared prosperity" part, and it's not decoration. It's the actual loop closing.

Owmi is the clearest real attempt at that. A cohort that wanted to learn product management built a working water-access platform for Lagos residents while learning — real interviews, a real prototype, a real business model. It got discontinued not because the idea failed, but because the operational weight of running a physical water-delivery service was more than the group could carry once the underlying interest had shifted toward digital products. Growth Clinic was the most direct, deliberate attempt to build this loop as an actual business — "individuals own a piece of the business as they build it to grow their skills, and they earn from the business as it grows and improves their community." That's almost word for word what I described to you as the goal, years before I described it to you again just now. Growth Clinic wound down at the end of 2025. The reason, as best I understand it myself: I couldn't find the right customer. The people I was pricing this training for wanted to make money, not learn for the sake of learning, and that made a training-first business model hard to sustain regardless of whether the underlying mechanism worked.

So the loop — group learns, group builds a real thing, group practises on it, group gets good, the thing sustains them and the people around them — has never actually closed all the way through, in anything I've built. Every piece up to "build something real while learning" has happened. The piece where that real thing sustainably generates shared prosperity hasn't been proven yet, every time I've tried it.

One more thing I don't want to lose in any version of this again: the early delivery was manual on purpose. Standing in a room, watching a cohort work, catching a mistake as it happened, adjusting on the spot — that's the same "fast feedback closes the loop" principle from the very beginning, just applied to how I was teaching it, not just to what was being taught. It wasn't a resource constraint I made peace with. It was the same mechanism, one level up.

---

Somewhere between the raw version of this and the last thing I wrote up, the language shifted. "We found a machine that turns ordinary community members into skilled, income-earning talent, and we want to build a business standing in the middle of that" turned into language about judgement, pattern recognition, execution intuition. None of that language is false. It's just not the point, and every time I made it sound more like a paper, it got easier to lose sight of what actually mattered — the entity people built, and whether it could sustain them, not how sharp their instincts got along the way.

The most recent real test of any of this is LORE — live right now, not a success case, unresolved. It's aimed at a version of the same watching-what-people-do-not-what-they-say principle, applied to organisations instead of communities. The first version got told plainly, by someone who saw it, that they'd rather just do the work manually. That's real feedback, and it's currently being answered with a rebuild rather than an argument. Two earlier tools — SYD Protocol and Slop Runner — never got as far as a real user at all. I built them to think the idea through out loud, not because anyone had asked for them.

I don't fully know yet which parts of the changes over the years were the idea actually maturing, and which were me smoothing off the parts that were harder to explain to people I was trying to impress rather than serve. I know the mechanism is real — the two-week dropoff of Learn by Doing, the 2018 pilot number, the AFF cohort's live realisation, the Five-Star Dilemma, all of it happened. What I'm less sure of is whether the "build a real thing that sustains the people who built it" half of the loop ever got the attention it needed, or whether I kept quietly drifting toward the individual-skill half because it was easier to explain and easier to sell.

That's the actual question sitting underneath all of this right now. Not what to call it. Whether the loop I originally set out to build was ever really the thing I ended up building.
