## Where it actually started

At Devcenter, part of what we did was connect developers in the community to clients who needed work done. I was the middleman — the project manager sitting between the client and whoever in the community had the skill to deliver. It sounds simple when I say it like that. In practice it kept breaking in the same place: we didn't have enough skilled people. Not enough to say yes to the clients we could have had, not enough to deliver well on the ones we did take. We were losing money and losing clients for the same reason — a talent gap, not a demand gap.

And then I started noticing something. People who'd been asking beginner questions in the community a few months earlier were now genuinely skilled. Not one or two of them. A pattern of them. And nobody had done anything special to make it happen. No one had pulled them aside, no extra training, no deliberate hand guiding it. It was just — happening. Organically, inside the community, on its own.

That's the moment the real idea showed up, before it had a name or a deck or a diagram attached to it.

If something was already teaching people that fast, without anyone designing it to, what if I could figure out what the actual mechanism was? Not admire it — take it apart. Find the gears. And if I could find the gears, I could build the same thing on purpose, and point it at more people, faster, or at specific people I actually needed skilled up for the work in front of us. If that worked, it wasn't just a fix for our talent problem. It meant you could take an ordinary community — any size — and turn it into a pipeline of skilled talent, and build a business standing exactly where I already was: in the middle, connecting that talent to the people who needed it. I remember thinking about it almost like how China built its wealth — not by having wealth first, but by building the industrial talent that let it manufacture everything the world needed. I wanted to do that for tech. For this new digital-infrastructure era. Except the raw material wasn't factories. It was people getting good, fast, inside a community that was already doing it without knowing it was doing it.

So I went looking for the mechanism.

What I found was learning by doing, but not in the vague way people usually mean that phrase. It was specific. People were contributing to open source projects — real ones, with real stakes attached, even if the stakes were just reputation inside the community. They'd take on a task. Ask a question when they got stuck. Someone further along would answer. They'd apply the answer, get feedback on what they'd built, fix it, and move to the next task already a little bit better than they were an hour before. Do that enough times inside the same tight loop and the project itself starts acting like an oven — you put in something half-formed, the heat of real feedback works on it, and what comes out the other side is more baked than what went in. Then you put the next thing in.

What made it fast wasn't any single piece of that loop. It was how quickly the loop closed. Ask a question, get an answer, back to work — all inside a community that was responsive enough that the gap between doing something wrong and knowing it was wrong stayed small. The learning wasn't happening despite the speed of the feedback. The speed of the feedback was the learning.

Once I saw it this clearly in our own community, I started seeing it everywhere I looked — other training programmes, other communities, all quietly running some version of the same loop without naming it either. That's a lot of what I later wrote about in the piece on turning communities into economies. But the seed of it, the actual discovery, was smaller and more specific than that piece makes it sound: it was watching a talent shortage inside one Lagos tech community solve itself, and refusing to just be grateful for it.

From there I did what felt like the obvious next step. I started pulling the mechanism apart properly — the learning-by-doing part, the fast-responding community part, the fact that the open source project itself had to be something people actually wanted to be seen contributing to, or the loop never got fed in the first place. Each of those felt like it could be studied and improved on its own. That's genuinely where the Growth and Innovation System came from — not as a framework I sat down to invent, but as labels I put on parts of a machine I'd already watched running, once I'd taken it apart far enough to see the parts separately.

And I think that's exactly where I need to be honest with myself, and with anyone reading this.

The raw diagram — problem, local contributors, informational resources, thinking frameworks, homegrown product — is the truest version of what I actually saw. But every time I turned it into a deck, or a pitch, or eventually a whitepaper, I was compressing it again. And compression isn't neutral. Each time, something got smoothed over to make it presentable, and I didn't always notice which thing. Somewhere in there, "we found a machine that turns ordinary community members into skilled, income-earning talent, fast, and we want to build a business standing in the middle of that" turned into language about judgement and pattern recognition and execution intuition — which isn't wrong, exactly, it's a real thing that happens inside the loop. But it was never the point. It was a side effect I ended up presenting as the headline, because it sounded more like something you could put in a paper.

I don't fully know yet which of those changes were the idea genuinely maturing, and which were me sanding off the parts that were harder to explain to an audience I was trying to impress rather than serve. That's the actual question I'm sitting with right now, more than "what should the finished version look like." Before I try to make this presentable again, I think I need to sit with that question honestly, rather than reach for the next format to put it in.
